Rabies is a deadly but preventable disease that kills more than 60,000 people each year, mostly children. Nearly all human infections are caused by bites from infected dogs. Vaccinating dogs is the most effective way to stop rabies from spreading, yet organized vaccination campaigns are rarely conducted in refugee settings, where free-roaming dogs are common and access to timely medical care after bites may be limited. We carried out a four-day mass dog vaccination campaign in the Kutupalong-Balukhali refugee settlement in Cox’s Bazar, Bangladesh. Using real-time data to guide field activities, we vaccinated over 78% of the dogs encountered during the campaign, most of which were unowned community dogs. We also surveyed residents to assess their knowledge of rabies and their experiences with dog bites. Awareness of rabies and appropriate wound care was limited, and dog bites were frequently reported. Our findings demonstrate that mass dog vaccination can be delivered in complex humanitarian settings and integrated into emergency health responses to reduce the risk of a preventable and fatal disease.
